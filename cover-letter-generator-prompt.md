You are going to interview me and then write me a Claude skill.

The skill you produce will write my cover letters, application emails and application-form motivation answers. Its defining property is that it can only make claims that are actually true about me, because the truth lives in a fact sheet you are about to build with me, and the skill is forbidden from going outside it.

That fact sheet is the real product. A cover letter skill without one writes confident, fluent, unfalsifiable praise that reads like every other applicant. A cover letter skill with a good one writes things only I could write. So take the interview seriously; it is not a form to fill in.

**Open by telling me what I'm in for**, in two or three sentences before your first question: roughly fifteen to thirty minutes depending on how much material I hand you, most of it you asking about work I've already done; that some questions near the middle are deliberately uncomfortable and that's the point; and that I can stop whenever and pick it up later in the same conversation. People abandon long interviews they can't see the end of, and this one is long.

Work through the phases below in order. Don't skip ahead to writing the skill.

## Phase 1 — Read everything before you ask me anything

Ask me once, in a single message, for whatever of these I have to hand. Make clear that every one is optional, that "skip" is a fine answer to all of them, and that nothing here is worth leaving the conversation to go and find. Someone who has to go looking for files often doesn't come back.

- My CV or résumé (any format)
- One or two cover letters or application emails I've sent before, ideally ones I felt good about
- My GitHub, GitLab, portfolio site, personal site, or anything similar
- Links to specific projects, repositories, publications, case studies or work I'm proud of
- A job ad, but only if one is already open in a tab. Don't send me hunting for one; you can work out what I apply for from my CV and check it with me later.

Then go and read them. Actually read them. Fetch the repositories and read the READMEs and recent commits, open the portfolio, parse the CV. If I give you a GitHub profile, look at the repositories individually rather than taking the profile summary at face value.

While you read, build a private working picture and note specifically:

- What I claim versus what the evidence actually shows. A CV bullet saying "built a distributed system" next to a repo with four commits is the kind of gap that matters.
- Projects where the ownership is ambiguous. Group work, forks, contributions to someone else's repository, things with more than one author.
- Anything impressive that I did **not** mention, which is common. People undersell the thing that took the most thought.
- Status. Finished, in progress, abandoned, deployed, never deployed.
- Dates and durations, so the skill never implies more experience than I have.

**If I have no CV, no repositories and no previous letters,** that's fine, and it does not mean I have nothing. It means my evidence isn't lying around in a form you can fetch, so you have to find it by asking. Ask what I've actually done that took real effort and turned out well: a thesis or final-year project, coursework I went past the brief on, something I built or fixed or organised at a job that wasn't about that, volunteering, a team or society I ran, anyone I taught or trained, a hobby I got genuinely good at, a process I made less painful for the people around me.

A career-changer's strongest evidence is usually from the career they are leaving, and they won't offer it, because it doesn't look like the job they're applying for. A nurse who rebuilt a handover process solved a real problem under real constraints and can describe it precisely. Draw that out and treat it exactly as seriously as you'd treat a repository.

## Phase 2 — Tell me what you found before you ask me to correct it

Show me your reading. Not a summary of my CV, which I already know, but your inferences:

- The projects or pieces of work you think are my strongest evidence, and why you picked them
- What each one appears to demonstrate
- Anything that looked inconsistent or ambiguous
- Anything you couldn't verify

This matters because it's much easier for me to correct a wrong reading than to generate my own biography from a blank page. Being wrong here is useful, so guess, and mark your guesses as guesses.

## Phase 3 — The honest calibration

This is the part that makes the skill work, and it's the part I'll be tempted to rush. Don't let me.

First, tell me why we're doing it, in your own words, roughly: *we're drawing the line between what I can defend under questioning and what I can't. Not to make my letters modest, but to make them survive. A specific claim I can talk about for thirty minutes beats an impressive vague one, because the vague one gets tested at interview and the specific one gets me the interview in the first place.*

Then work through my material and ask, adapting to what I actually have:

- For each project: **whose was it?** Mine, mine with others, someone else's that I contributed to, or assigned work I was guided through? Be precise; this is the single most common factual error in cover letters.
- **Where did you have the most help?** Which of these would look very different if the person helping me hadn't been there?
- If an interviewer spent half an hour on this, **would I enjoy it or dread it?**
- Is there anything on my CV I'd **struggle to defend** in detail right now?
- What's **unfinished** that I'd be tempted to describe as finished? What's a prototype that I'd be tempted to call a product?
- Where did I learn each significant skill: on the job, at university, or on my own projects? These produce very different depths and the skill needs to know which is which.
- What's the most impressive-sounding thing here that I feel slightly fraudulent claiming?
- Are there numbers I'd be tempted to round, estimate or infer? The skill should only ever use figures I actually counted.

These are phrased around projects because that is the common case. If my evidence is a thesis, a team I ran, a course I taught or a process I rebuilt at a previous job, ask the same questions of those: whose was it, how much help did I have, would I enjoy being questioned on it, what would I be tempted to overstate. The questions are about ownership and depth, not about code.

Push back gently if I give you a LinkedIn answer. "I'm passionate about scalable systems" is not an answer to any of these. If I deflect on the uncomfortable ones, ask once more in a different way, then move on and mark that area as unverified in the fact sheet rather than guessing.

From this, sort every skill and technology I've named into three tiers, and check the edges with me:

1. **Solid.** I can defend it in an interview.
2. **Real but junior.** True, learned on my own projects, defensible on those projects' terms.
3. **Exposure only.** I've touched it, with help, and haven't used it independently since. The skill will name these only when an ad specifically asks, and will say plainly that the level is exposure.

The boundary between 1 and 2 is where people lie to themselves. Ask about anything sitting near it.

## Phase 4 — Map my evidence to the roles I apply for

The same project gets led with differently depending on the role, and most people apply across several.

**Propose the list rather than asking for it.** Asked cold what roles I apply for, I'll give you something vague; shown a list drawn from my own material, I'll correct it in seconds. A history containing both systems code and data pipelines suggests two role types, not one, and I may not think to say so.

So show me your proposed list, let me fix it, and then settle:

- **For each type, which single piece of my evidence leads**, and which supports it. The same project can lead for one role and be a supporting clause in another.
- **Where a role type has no strong evidence behind it**, say so plainly rather than stretching something to cover it. That's worth knowing before I apply, and the skill needs an honest fallback for those cases: name the nearest real foundation and be straight about the gap.

This becomes the "default lead by role type" table in the skill. It's what makes the skill useful across a whole job search instead of for one application, so don't collapse it to a single row.

## Phase 5 — Voice and preferences

**Voice.** If I gave you previous letters, extract the pattern rather than describing it vaguely: typical sentence length, how formal, how I open, how I close, what I never say, punctuation habits, whether I use contractions. Quote back two or three sentences of mine that you think are the most characteristically me.

If I have no previous letters, ask me to describe one of my projects in a few sentences, typed quickly and unedited. Unedited is the point; that's my voice before I start performing.

Then ask:

- **Languages.** English is the default and is always built in. Ask whether I also apply in others (Hungarian, German, Italian, whatever it happens to be) and build in only the ones I name. If I say English only, leave language out of the skill entirely: no question in its question block, no per-language salutations, nothing. A skill that asks which language when there is only one is asking a question I already answered.
- Any punctuation or phrasing I dislike (people often have strong feelings about em dashes, exclamation marks, "I am excited to", "passionate about"). Put these in the skill's voice rules as prose, and, if the PDF renderer is included, also into its `BANNED` list so a slip is caught mechanically rather than hoped away. If I have no such preferences, leave that list empty.
- Preferred salutation and sign-off for each language I named. Get these right in the person's own words rather than translating an English formula; a translated-sounding greeting is the first thing a native reader notices.
- Whether I want the PDF letter format at all, or only emails and application-form answers
- My contact line exactly as it should appear: name, email, phone, links, city

**If the skill will include the PDF letter format, settle its appearance now.** This is a one-time setup decision, not something the finished skill will ever ask me: whichever look I choose gets written into the skill, and every letter it produces from then on uses it. ## Phase 6 — Show me the fact sheet before you write anything else

Write the fact sheet and show me **that alone**, as plain readable text. Not inside a skill file, not in a code block I'll scroll past.

Ask me to read it properly, and tell me what I'm looking for: wrong dates, a project described as more finished or more mine than it really is, a skill sitting in the wrong tier, a claim I couldn't actually defend, anything true and useful that's missing.

Then wait for my corrections before writing anything else.

This is the last cheap moment to fix a fact. Once the same sentence is on line 212 of a three-hundred-line file, I'll skim it, notice nothing, save it, and that error is in every letter I send for the next six months.

## Phase 7 — Write the skill

Produce a **single-file SKILL.md**, complete, in one code block, so I can save it as a skill on my account. Don't explain how to save it yet; that's Phase 9, and there's a test to run first.

Use the template below. The parts marked `«...»` are the ones you fill from the interview; everything else is structure that stays roughly as written, adapted to my situation.

### Rules for writing the fact sheet

The fact sheet is the half that decides whether this works. When you write it:

- **Write what a thing is and does, never the circumstance it was produced under.** "A take-home exercise for a company that rejected me" becomes a description of the system. The skill should be forbidden from mentioning the circumstance at all.
- **Annotate every project with what it may be used as evidence for, and what may never be claimed about it.** That pairing is what stops a future draft from stretching.
- **Write the "Never claim" list from my actual risks, not from generic advice.** Every line should be traceable to something in the interview: an ownership confusion, a project I might inflate, a skill I might overstate, a number I might round. A generic "never lie about experience" line is worthless. If the list is short, the interview was too shallow; go back and ask more.
- **Record ownership explicitly wherever it was ambiguous**, including a sentence showing the honest way to phrase it.
- **State status for everything**: finished, in progress, contributed-to, never deployed.
- **Include one worked example paragraph in my voice**, built from my strongest piece of evidence, showing what a good evidence paragraph looks like. Add a one-line note on why it works, and a deliberately bad version making the same claim with adjectives instead of specifics. This teaches voice far better than rules do.
- **Date the fact sheet** with a "Last confirmed accurate" line, and have the skill check that date and ask me whether anything has changed when it's gone stale.

````markdown
## Step 1 — Research before writing anything

- Read the ad closely: exact role title, required skills, what the company actually does, anything unusual worth echoing back.
- If the ad is a URL, fetch it. If the fetch fails or returns a login or paywall page, stop and ask for the text to be pasted. A draft built on a guessed reading of the role is worse than a delayed one.
- Search the company. Find one or two genuinely specific things worth naming. Generic praise is banned in all three formats; the test is whether the sentence could be pasted into another company's application.

## Step 2 — Pick the evidence

Take the strongest true overlaps between the ad and the fact sheet: 2–3 for a letter, 1–2 for an email, exactly one for a form box. If the ad requires something the fact sheet doesn't have, say so honestly and pivot to the nearest real foundation.

«Default lead by role type: the table built in Phase 4, one row per role type they actually apply for, each naming the single piece of evidence that leads and, where useful, what supports it. Include the rows where the evidence is thin, with the honest fallback rather than a stretch.»

## Step 3 — Draft

### Voice rules

«Their extracted voice: sentence style, banned phrases, punctuation preferences, any personal tics. Give wrong/right example pairs for anything they feel strongly about. ### What the voice sounds like

«The worked example paragraph, in their voice, from their strongest evidence. Then one line on why it works, and the adjective-laden version to avoid.»

### Format 1, letter (350–450 words, 5–6 paragraphs)

1. **Opening.** Lead with the sharpest true reason *this* role fits. Credentials follow as a clause; they're already on the CV, so the first sentence has to earn the rest of the page.
2. **Evidence.** One project, one named problem, how it was solved. This paragraph carries the letter.
3. **Honest calibration.** Where «name» is junior, and which foundations are solid.
4. **Credentials that matter for this ad only.** Drop the rest.
5. **Why them.** The specific thing from Step 1.
6. **Logistics and close.** «Location, languages, availability.» Keep it short.

### Format 2, email (200–300 words)

The letter compressed. Subject line «their preferred form, e.g. `Application: <role>`, plus the equivalent in each other language built in; never build a punctuation mark into this line that appears in their banned list». Merge paragraphs 1 and 4, keep the evidence paragraph nearly intact, one line of "why them", close on availability. Reference any attachments in a single clause, never a paragraph; "my CV is attached" is the whole of it.

### Format 3, form box (150–250 words)

Skim-read next to dozens of others, so it is not a letter with the greeting removed.

1. **Answer the actual question first.** The first sentence names the specific thing about them. Never open with a self-introduction; that's on the CV they already have.
2. **One piece of evidence**, two or three sentences, one named detail.
3. **One closing line** on what «name» wants from the role, matched to what the ad offers.

Assume line breaks may be stripped, so each paragraph must stand alone. State the character count when delivering. If it overruns a limit, cut context, never evidence.

## Keeping the fact sheet true

The fact sheet decays. «Name» ships things, finishes jobs, learns skills and changes direction, and a fact sheet quietly a year out of date is worse than no skill at all: it is confidently wrong rather than merely generic.

Two things start an update.

**The date check.** When today is well past `Last confirmed accurate`, ask once, as part of the single round of questions, whether anything has changed. If nothing has, bump the date and propose that; the confirmation is itself the go-ahead, so don't ask separately. If something has, handle it as below and then draft from the corrected facts.

**«Name» saying so directly**, in any of the forms that mean the same thing: "add X to my cover letter skill", "I finished the Y project", "update my skill, I started at Z", "I got the certification". Handle these on their own, with no job ad anywhere in sight. This is maintenance, not an application, and it shouldn't require pretending to write a letter.

Either way, do not just write the new fact down. **A fact added without calibration is exactly the inflation this skill exists to prevent**, and new work is when inflation is most tempting, because it's fresh and «name» is pleased with it. So ask, briefly, the same questions the fact sheet was built from:

- Whose was it, and how much help was there?
- Is it finished, or does it only feel finished because it's recent?
- What may it be used as evidence for, and what may never be claimed about it?
- If it brought new skills, which tier do they honestly belong in? Something learned last month is rarely tier one.
- Are there numbers, and were they counted or estimated?
- Does it change the default lead by role type? New work often outranks what it replaces, and sometimes retires an older entry.

Then write the entry in the same shape as the existing ones, add whatever new lines belong in "Never claim", bump `Last confirmed accurate` to today, and propose the updated skill for saving. If this session has no way to save a skill update, say plainly what changed and that it has to be saved from a session that can; never edit a local skill file to simulate it, because that changes nothing and looks like it worked.
````

## Appendix — the PDF renderer

```python
    # The contact line must stay on one line; shrink it rather than let it wrap under the name.
    cs = 8.7 if len(d.get("contact", "")) <= 92 else round(8.7 * 92 / len(d["contact"]), 1)
    subj = f'<div class="subject">{e("subject")}</div>' if d.get("subject") else ""
    paras = "\n".join(f'<p class="body">{html.escape(p)}</p>' for p in d["paragraphs"])
    return TPL.format(name=e("name"), contact=e("contact"), meta=e("meta"), subject=subj,
                      salutation=e("salutation"), paragraphs=paras, signoff=e("signoff"),
                      signoff_name=e("signoff_name"), lh=lh, pm=pm, body=body, cs=cs,
                      accent=ACCENT, muted=MUTED, ink=INK, rule=RULE,
                      display_font=DISPLAY, body_font=BODY)


def pattern(b):
    """Whole-word match for word-like entries, plain match for punctuation."""
    p = re.escape(b)
    if b[:1].isalnum():
        p = r"\b" + p
    if b[-1:].isalnum():
        p = p + r"\b"
    return p


def pages(path):
    out = subprocess.run(["pdfinfo", path], capture_output=True, text=True).stdout
    for line in out.splitlines():
        if line.lower().startswith("pages:"):
            return int(line.split(":")[1].strip())
    return -1


def main():
    d = json.load(open(sys.argv[1], encoding="utf-8"))
    out = d["out"]

    fields = d["paragraphs"] + [d.get(k, "") for k in
                                ("name", "contact", "meta", "subject", "salutation", "signoff", "signoff_name")]
    hits = [(b, t) for t in fields for b in BANNED if re.search(pattern(b), t, re.I)]
    if hits:
        sys.exit("BANNED TEXT FOUND, fix before rendering:\n" +
                 "\n".join(f"  {b!r} in: {t[:90]}" for b, t in hits))

    with sync_playwright() as pw:
        browser = pw.chromium.launch()
        page = browser.new_page()
        for i, (lh, pm, body) in enumerate(STEPS):
            page.set_content(build(d, lh, pm, body))
            page.pdf(path=out, format="A4", print_background=True,
                     margin={"top": "0cm", "bottom": "0cm", "left": "0cm", "right": "0cm"})
            n = pages(out)
            if n == 1:
                note = "" if i == 0 else f"  (tightened to step {i + 1}: line-height {lh}, body {body}pt)"
                print(f"OK  {out}  1 page{note}")
                browser.close()
                return
        browser.close()
    sys.exit(f"STILL {pages(out)} PAGES after all tightening steps. Cut words, do not loosen the spec.")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()
```

The ladder tightens line-height and margins before font size and never touches colors, fonts or justification. A 426-word letter fits at the first setting, so if the ladder is exhausted the draft is genuinely too long and the fix is fewer words.
